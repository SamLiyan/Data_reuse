Non-host reads in blue catfish reproductive tissue

What two deposited RNA-seq studies contain besides fish, and whether it has anything to do with how the offspring do.

Week 12 · Data Reuse · two BioProjects, 74 runs, one species · public data only

An RNA-seq experiment is run to measure the expression of one animal's genes. The reads that do not map to that animal are dropped at the first step of every pipeline and never looked at again. They are not noise in any strict sense — they are sequence from whatever else was in the tube — but because they answer no question the experiment was designed around, they are discarded.

This report takes two blue catfish (Ictalurus furcatus) studies and asks what is in that discarded fraction, whether it differs between fish, and whether it has any relationship to the thing a hatchery actually cares about.

Bacterial sequence is present in every library in both studies. Viruses and fungi are negligible throughout (maxima of 0.0056% and 0.0026%), so the non-host fraction is, for practical purposes, bacterial.

The two tissues are not alike. Testis libraries sit in a narrow band around 2.2%. Sperm libraries run from 0.18% to 85.6% — a 483-fold range within one species, one tissue and one set of samples (Mann–Whitney p = 1.7 × 10⁻⁵). One run, SRR33411471, is 85.6% bacterial against 4.7% metazoan: the majority of that library is not the animal it was submitted as.

3.Whether it is the same organism every time

A quantity of bacterial sequence on its own says little. Sequence introduced by something all the samples passed through — water, reagents, surfaces, the hands that stripped the fish — should show the same organisms in every library, because every library met the same source. Sequence carried by the animal should differ between animals. So the diagnostic is not how much there is but how consistent the dominant organism is.

Neither study behaves like a single shared source. The most frequent dominant genus in sperm, Parabacteroides, holds the top position in only 13 of 60 libraries, and 14 different genera take that position across the study.

The identity depends on the amount

Classifying each library's most abundant genus against two reference lists fixed before the analysis — one of genera repeatedly reported from laboratory and environmental sources, one of taxa reported from teleost gut and mucosa — splits the sperm libraries cleanly by how loaded they are.

Environment genus on topGut genus on top Other
≤ 30% bacterial19315
> 30% bacterial11102

Fisher exact p = 0.0026. Libraries whose most abundant organism is a gut genus carry a median 49% bacterial reads; those topped by an environment-associated genus, 18%. The lightly loaded libraries are headed by Stenotrophomonas, Pseudomonas, Staphylococcus, Cutibacterium and Stutzerimonas; the heavily loaded ones by Parabacteroides and relatives.

Three libraries are topped by Alistipes, Blautia and Escherichia, which are gut organisms in the literature but were not on the list fixed before the analysis. They are counted under “other” rather than reassigned after the result was seen. Including them would strengthen the association reported above.

4.The same male, sampled twice

The sperm study has a feature that makes the rest of this possible: each of the 30 males was sequenced twice, once from fresh milt and once after cryopreservation. Two questions follow, and they have different answers.

ComparisonStatisticResult
Fresh vs cryopreserved, pairedWilcoxon p = 0.685 No difference. Medians 17.2% and 18.1%.
Fresh vs cryo, within male ρ = +0.742, p = 2.6e-06 Strongly correlated.

Freezing does not change how much of a library is bacterial. But two libraries prepared separately, from physically different material taken from the same fish, agree closely on it. The quantity is therefore a property of the individual male rather than of the handling, and — relevant to what follows — it has been measured twice rather than once.

5.Whether it relates to offspring performance

These males were not only sequenced. They were spawned, and the hatchery recorded hatch success and larval survival at 30 days for each cross. The discarded fraction can therefore be tested against an outcome.

The crosses were made with fresh milt, so the 30 fresh libraries are the analysis. The cryopreserved libraries come from the same males and are tabulated alongside as a repeat measurement of the predictor; they are not a second test.

Hatch success

ρ = -0.096, p = 0.614, r² = 0.001. There is no relationship. A male whose milt library is 0.18% bacterial and a male whose library is 79% bacterial hatch about equally well. The predictor varies over nearly three orders of magnitude and accounts for none of the variance in the outcome.

Larval survival at 30 days

ρ = +0.331, p = 0.074, r² = 0.060. This does not reach significance either. The correlation is weak, it does not clear p < 0.05 at n = 30, and no relationship is claimed for this trait.

Neither outcome is predicted by the non-host fraction. The quantity varies by nearly three orders of magnitude between males and is reproducible within a male across two preparations — but across these 30 fish it carries no detectable information about hatch success or larval survival.

That is worth stating plainly rather than softening. A predictor can be large, structured and repeatable without being related to the outcome anyone cares about, and at n = 30 this study is not powered to find a small effect if one exists.

6.What was checked, and what cannot be

CheckResultReading
Sequencing depth vs non-host fraction ρ = -0.014, p = 0.941 Depth does not drive the predictor.
Hatch success vs larval survival ρ = +0.466, p = 0.009 Related but not interchangeable; neither tracks the predictor.
Host fraction vs non-host fraction ρ ≈ −0.99 The limiting problem, below.

The third row is the serious one, and it applies to the predictor itself rather than to any result. Within a single library, “more bacterial reads” and “less fish RNA recovered” are not two measurements but one, expressed as a share of the same total. So even the between-male variation in sections 2 to 4 is ambiguous: it may reflect the microbial population in the milt, or how much host RNA came out of that male's sample, which would make it a proxy for sample quality, cell content or handling. A read fraction cannot distinguish these. Doing so requires an absolute measure, a spike-in or 16S qPCR, which deposited RNA-seq does not carry.

Other limits worth stating plainly:

  • Neither study deposited a blank extraction. Without a matched negative control, the separation of environment-associated from animal-associated sequence rests on a published genus list and on the consistency argument in section 3.
  • Gut organisms in a gonad or milt sample have more than one explanation — translocation, or contact during dissection and stripping. Nothing here distinguishes them.

7.Conclusion

Both studies contain substantial sequence that is not the animal, and in sperm the amount varies between individuals by nearly three orders of magnitude. That variation is not procedural: it is reproducible within a male across two separate preparations, and its composition shifts with its magnitude, from environment-associated genera in the light libraries to gut anaerobes in the heavy ones.

Tested against outcomes, it predicts neither hatch success nor larval survival at 30 days. Whatever this variation represents, it does not translate into the measures a hatchery records, at least not at the sample size available.

The narrower point is the one that generalises. Every library in both studies carried this information at the time of submission, at no additional cost, and it was discarded before the first analysis step.
